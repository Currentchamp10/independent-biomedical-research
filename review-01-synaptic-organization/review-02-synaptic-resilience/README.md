# Review 02 — Synaptic Resilience in Alzheimer’s Disease

## The Question Behind This Review

How do synapses preserve or restore function despite Alzheimer’s disease pathology, and what determines whether these compensatory mechanisms succeed or eventually fail?

## Overview

This review examines synaptic resilience in Alzheimer’s disease through the relationship between pathological stress and the capacity of neurons and surrounding cellular systems to preserve synaptic function.

Rather than focusing only on synaptic loss, the analysis considers what happens to the synapses that remain, with particular attention to compensatory structural remodelling, glutamate receptor organization, membrane homeostasis, proteostasis, autophagy, microglial responses, and molecular signatures associated with preserved cognition.

## Major Areas of Analysis

* Cognitive and synaptic resilience despite Alzheimer’s pathology
* Aβ-associated disruption of synaptic organization and receptor trafficking
* Tau-associated changes in synaptic protein regulation
* Proteostasis and autophagy in synaptic maintenance
* Structural compensation and dendritic spine regeneration
* Microglial and TREM2-dependent regulation of damaged synapses
* Molecular and cellular signatures associated with resilience
* The transition from compensation to synaptic and cognitive failure

## Experimental Approaches Discussed

The review considers evidence obtained using:

* Two-photon and high-resolution microscopy
* Dendritic spine imaging
* Super-resolution microscopy
* Electrophysiology
* Proteomics and peptidomics
* Transcriptomics and single-cell approaches
* Immunofluorescence and biochemical analyses
* Experimental Alzheimer’s disease models
* Human post-mortem tissue and longitudinal biomarker studies

## Central Research Question

How do membrane organization, cellular maintenance systems, and structural synaptic plasticity interact to determine whether an Alzheimer’s-affected synapse is lost, preserved, or functionally compensated?
